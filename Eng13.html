“Happy birthday,” “selamat hari jadi,” or “sanah helwah” have become customary greetings around the world. It is rare to come across someone who does not celebrate their birthday; perhaps they are antisocial. But do you know how this tradition began? A wise person would certainly not simply follow a practice without taking the time to learn about the origins of the celebration they observe. Therefore, let us take a journey through the history of birthdays.

Ancient Sumer, 2,400 BC

Some say that the celebration of birthdays was started by Pharaoh, based on Genesis 40:20 in the Bible. No, it actually dates back much further—around 4,400 years ago, in 2400 BC, in ancient Sumer.

Professor Vladimir V. Emelianov, a scholar of history at Saint Petersburg State University, in his 2017 journal article entitled “First Account of a Birthday in Human History”, states that the first person known to have celebrated a birthday was a Sumerian king named Lugalanda. Five discovered Sumerian administrative tablets record that Lugalanda celebrated the birthdays of his children by preparing various kinds of lavish food as offerings to the idol of the goddess Bau.

Besides seeking Bau’s protection for his children, he did so to convince the people that his lineage had been chosen by the gods and goddesses. This earliest recorded birthday celebration in history shows that it was practised only by the elite, not as a celebration, but as a religious ritual and a display of power. From here, we will explore how the ritual popularised by Lugalanda evolved into a celebration, as well as the innovations that transformed it into the celebration we know today.

Ancient Egypt, 2,000 BC

Now, let us return to the account in Genesis 40:20, which mentions the Pharaoh’s birthday celebration. The findings of Professor Vladimir V. Emelianov show that the pharaohs did not initiate it, but continued the practice of Lugalanda, around 400 years later, or 2,000 BC.

If the Sumerian kings celebrated birthdays to seek protection from idols for their descendants, the Pharaohs, on the other hand, celebrated them to remind the people of their status as living idols. According to Andrew Collins, a scholar of history at the University of Queensland, in his article published in 2014 in the journal The Classical Quarterly, when a Pharaoh was crowned, his status was elevated from an ordinary human being to a god. Unlike the Sumerian practice of using lavish food as offerings to idols, the Pharaohs held grand feasts for the people of their court.

According to an article by Anthony Spalinger, an Egyptologist at the University of Auckland, published in 1995 in the Journal of Near Eastern Studies, entitled “Some Remarks on the Epagomenal Days in Ancient Egypt”, besides the Pharaohs, the people of ancient Egypt also celebrated the birthdays of their gods, such as Osiris, Horus, Seth, Isis, and Nephthys. Here, we can see how ancient Egypt innovated the celebration of birthdays, from celebrating royal bloodlines to venerating gods and humans regarded as manifestations of gods.

Ancient Persia, 450 BC

Ancient Persian society, around 450 BC, which at the time followed the Magian religion, played an important role in the development of modern birthday celebrations. Herodotus, the ancient Greek historian known as the Father of History, recorded a unique custom in the Achaemenid Empire in his book, The Histories, stating that every Persian celebrated their birthdays, not just the king, by holding feasts. This marked a major revolution in the history of birthday celebrations. And they were also pioneers in the practice of giving birthday gifts.

Ancient Greece, 450 BC

Around 450 BC as well, according to Athenaeus, an ancient Greek writer and grammarian, in his book The Deipnosophists, ancient Greece was the first to incorporate cakes, candles, and wishes into birthday celebrations. Unlike the Persians, who celebrated personal birthdays, the Greeks only celebrated the birthdays of their gods, particularly the moon goddess, Artemis. The cake used as an offering was called amphiphon. It was round in shape, in keeping with the moon. And the candles represented the stars. The smoke from the candles was said to carry wishes to the heavens, where Artemis could hear them.

Roman Civilisation, 100 BC

Kathryn Argetsinger, a Classics lecturer at the University of Rochester, wrote in her 1992 article in the journal Classical Antiquity, entitled “Birthday Rituals: Friends and Patrons in Roman Poetry and Cult”, that around 100 BC, Roman society celebrated the birthdays of emperors and ordinary people with religious rituals and social gatherings. Furthermore, they commemorated the days of temple dedications, the founding of cities, and the deaths of figures and legends from the past. Most of the forms of celebration we observe today are inherited from Roman traditions. They were also the ones who initiated the New Year celebration and public holiday on 1 January, dedicated to the god Janus, around 45 BC.

Christmas in Rome, 336 AD

In the third century, Origen of Alexandria, a Christian theologian, criticised birthday celebrations, based on biblical accounts showing that birthdays were celebrations observed by wicked figures such as Pharaoh and Herod. Moreover, there was not a single account showing them being practised by the Prophets or righteous people of the Abrahamic faiths. It was also opposed by prominent figures of early Christianity such as Tertullian, Arnobius, and Lactantius.

However, the celebration that was initially rejected by Christian scholars was eventually incorporated into the religion by the religious authorities themselves—the Church. According to Paul Bradshaw, a scholar of Christian liturgy, in his 2020 article “The Dating of Christmas”, on 25 December, 336 AD, approximately 300 years after Prophet ‘Isa (AS), the world's biggest and most popular birthday celebration was born in Rome—Christmas.

Various theories have emerged because there is no historical record showing that 25 December was the actual birth date of the Son of Mary (AS). Since the date coincides with Dies Natalis Solis Invicti, a festival of the sun-god Sol Invictus in pagan Roman religion, some claim that it was chosen to replace that festival. Others argue that, because pagans were very fond of celebrating birthdays, the Church created Christmas to attract them to the Christian faith. What is certain is that there is no consensus among historians as to why the Church chose this date and made it a celebration.

Mawlid in Egypt, 517 AH

Beginning with a Sumerian king who worshipped idols, continued by the Pharaohs, innovated by the Magians of Persia, worshippers of the Greek gods, the pagan Romans, and Christians—eventually, birthday celebrations influenced the Muslims. In his book, Al-Khitat al-Maqriziyyah, Taqiuddin al-Maqrizi (RH), a prominent Muslim historian of the Mamluk era, stated that the earliest recorded celebration of the Prophet’s birthday that can be traced was made an official celebration in 517 AH, during the rule of the Fatimid dynasty in Egypt, a state adhering to Ismaili Shi‘ism.

Germany, 19th Century

Elizabeth H. Pleck, a Professor of History at the University of Illinois, in her 2000 book Celebrating the Family: Ethnicity, Consumer Culture, and Family Rituals, states that modern birthday celebrations originated from the children’s festival Kinderfest in Germany in the early 19th century. German immigrants in the United States were responsible for bringing the tradition that is now widely practised: placing candles corresponding to the person’s age on a cake and making a wish before blowing them out.

Hollywood and the American mass media, with their global influence, subsequently revived pagan birthday celebrations, making them mainstream and spreading them throughout the world. The increasingly secular and liberal Western lifestyle of the modern age has caused people around the world to forget that birthday celebrations were originally always religious in nature. Muslims, who once merely celebrated the Prophet’s birthday, now flock to celebrate their own birthdays.

The Ruling on Birthday Celebrations

The earliest Mawlid celebration pioneered by the Shi‘a only appeared 500 years after the death of Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, while widespread personal birthday celebrations among Muslims emerged 1,000 years later. Since then, various fatwas have been issued on both, either permitting or prohibiting them. And we are responsible for seeking the answer that is most correct, not one that satisfies our desires or because we fear that society will reject us.
