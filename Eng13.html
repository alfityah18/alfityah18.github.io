“Happy birthday,” “selamat hari jadi,” or “sanah helwah” have become customary greetings around the world. It is rare to come across someone who does not celebrate their birthday; perhaps they are antisocial. But do you know how this tradition began? A wise person would certainly not simply follow a practice without taking the time to learn about the origins of the celebration they observe. Therefore, let us take a journey through the history of birthdays.

Ancient Sumer, 2,400 BC

Some say that the celebration of birthdays was started by Pharaoh, based on Genesis 40:20 in the Bible. No, it actually dates back much further—around 4,400 years ago, in 2400 BC, in ancient Sumer.

Professor Vladimir V. Emelianov, a scholar of history at Saint Petersburg State University, in his 2017 journal article entitled “First Account of a Birthday in Human History”, states that the first person known to have celebrated a birthday was a Sumerian king named Lugalanda. Five discovered Sumerian administrative tablets record that Lugalanda celebrated the birthdays of his children by preparing various kinds of lavish food as offerings to the idol of the goddess Bau.

Besides seeking Bau’s protection for his children, he did so to convince the people that his lineage had been chosen by the gods and goddesses. This earliest recorded birthday celebration in history shows that it was practised only by the elite, not as a celebration, but as a religious ritual and a display of power. From here, we will explore how the ritual popularised by Lugalanda evolved into a celebration, as well as the innovations that transformed it into the celebration we know today.

Ancient Egypt, 2,000 BC

Now, let us return to the account in Genesis 40:20, which mentions the Pharaoh’s birthday celebration. The findings of Professor Vladimir V. Emelianov show that the pharaohs did not initiate it, but continued the practice of Lugalanda, around 400 years later, or 2,000 BC.

If the Sumerian kings celebrated birthdays to seek protection from idols for their descendants, the Pharaohs, on the other hand, celebrated them to remind the people of their status as living idols. According to Andrew Collins, a scholar of history at the University of Queensland, in his article published in 2014 in the journal The Classical Quarterly, when a Pharaoh was crowned, his status was elevated from an ordinary human being to a god. Unlike the Sumerian practice of using lavish food as offerings to idols, the Pharaohs held grand feasts for the people of their court.

According to an article by Anthony Spalinger, an Egyptologist at the University of Auckland, published in 1995 in the Journal of Near Eastern Studies, entitled “Some Remarks on the Epagomenal Days in Ancient Egypt”, besides the Pharaohs, the people of ancient Egypt also celebrated the birthdays of their gods, such as Osiris, Horus, Seth, Isis, and Nephthys. Here, we can see how ancient Egypt innovated the celebration of birthdays, from celebrating royal bloodlines to venerating gods and humans regarded as manifestations of gods.

Ancient Persia, 450 BC

Ancient Persian society, around 450 BC, which at the time followed the Magian religion, played an important role in the development of modern birthday celebrations. Herodotus, the ancient Greek historian known as the Father of History, recorded a unique custom in the Achaemenid Empire in his book, The Histories, stating that everyone celebrated their birthdays, not just the king, by holding feasts. This marked a major revolution in the history of birthday celebrations. And they were also pioneers in the practice of giving birthday gifts.

Ancient Greece, 450 BC

Around 450 BC as well, according to Athenaeus, an ancient Greek writer and grammarian, in his book The Deipnosophists, ancient Greece was the first to incorporate cakes, candles, and wishes into birthday celebrations. Unlike the Persians, the Greeks celebrated the birthdays of their gods, particularly the moon goddess, Artemis. The cake used as an offering was called amphiphon. It was round in shape, in keeping with the moon. And the candles represented the stars. The smoke from the candles was said to carry wishes to the heavens, where Artemis could hear them.

Roman Civilisation, 100 BC

Around 100 BC, Roman civilisation spread birthday celebrations widely, incorporating elements of Sumerian power and religious rituals, Egyptian divinity, Persian personal celebrations with their “party” and gift-giving culture, as well as Greek cakes and candles along with their “wish”. It was celebrated with family, friends, and patrons, and later helped shape the birthday celebrations we know today. This can be seen in an article by Kathryn Argetsinger, a lecturer at the University of Rochester, published in 1992 in the journal Classical Antiquity.
